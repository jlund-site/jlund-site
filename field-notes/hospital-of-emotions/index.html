LOS ANGELES   /   JULY 2026   /   FIELD NOTE 002

If These Walls
Could Feel

Inside the Hospital of Emotions, where an old Los Angeles hospital becomes a playground for the imagination—and the past never feels entirely gone.

There is something unsettling about walking into a hospital when you aren't there to see a doctor. Even when you're perfectly healthy, the building itself seems to trigger something instinctive. The long corridors, the operating rooms, the unfamiliar spaces where people once waited for answers that could change their lives. Hospitals are places we enter carrying emotions we rarely experience anywhere else, and even after their doors close for the last time, I wonder whether some of those feelings remain.

I wasn't expecting to think about any of that when I arrived at the Hospital of Emotions in Los Angeles. I had come to experience an ambitious immersive art installation, the kind of creative experiment that immediately captures my imagination. But as I began exploring the former hospital, I found myself becoming just as fascinated by the building as I was by the extraordinary artwork filling its rooms.

There was something dark about the experience, almost dangerous, although I never felt that I was actually in danger. Perhaps it was the unfamiliarity of wandering through spaces that had once served such serious purposes, or the strange contrast between the building's medical history and the creative worlds that now occupied it. Whatever the reason, I couldn't stop thinking about the people who had been there before us.

I imagined doctors moving between operating rooms, nurses working through the night, and families sitting in waiting areas hoping for good news. I thought about the patients who had arrived frightened and uncertain, and the countless conversations that must have taken place behind those doors. Some of those moments would have been joyful, others heartbreaking, and many probably forgotten by everyone except the people who experienced them. Standing there, surrounded by art, I kept returning to one question: If these walls could talk, what stories would they tell?

What made the experience so fascinating was that the artists seemed to have been given permission to explore precisely the kind of emotional territory that the building suggested. Rather than erase the hospital's identity and transform it into a conventional gallery, the creative concept embraced the unusual setting. Rooms that once belonged to the medical world had become individual artistic environments, each with its own atmosphere, personality, and interpretation of human emotion.

At times, it felt as though Meow Wolf had checked into an abandoned hospital and been handed the keys to every room. There was that same sense of curiosity and unpredictability, the feeling that opening another door might lead somewhere entirely unexpected. Yet Hospital of Emotions had a different kind of intensity because its setting was so deeply connected to the subject matter. The artwork wasn't simply occupying an interesting building; it was unfolding inside a place where fear, hope, grief, love, and relief had once been part of everyday life.

I was especially taken by the artists themselves and the remarkable range of imagination they brought to the project. There is something exciting about bringing together creative people with different perspectives and giving them the freedom to interpret a shared idea without demanding that everything look or feel the same. Some spaces were playful and surreal, others darker and more introspective. Moving between them felt less like walking through a traditional exhibition and more like exploring the different ways people experience and express emotion.

I also loved the boldness of the concept. Taking an aging hospital and imagining it as a destination for contemporary art requires more than a good creative idea. It requires someone to recognize possibility in a place that others might overlook, along with the confidence to invite artists into an imperfect environment and allow the unexpected to happen. The result wasn't polished in the conventional sense, and that was part of its appeal. The building's character, its imperfections, and its history gave the experience an authenticity that would have been difficult to recreate in a purpose-built venue.

As I moved through the rooms, I found myself thinking about how we assign meaning to places. A hospital is designed around the human body, but so much of what happens inside one is emotional. It is where people receive life-changing news, where strangers offer comfort, where families come together, and where ordinary days can suddenly become extraordinary. There was something poetic about seeing those same spaces transformed into places where artists explored the very emotions that had always existed within them.

By the time I left, I realized that what stayed with me wasn't any single installation. It was the combination of the art, the people who created it, and the building that held it all together. The experience had been imaginative, occasionally unsettling, and wonderfully unconventional, but it had also made me curious about the lives that had passed through those rooms long before the artists arrived.

I don't know what stories the hospital's walls would tell if they could speak. Perhaps they would describe moments of extraordinary courage, unexpected kindness, or the quiet resilience of people facing something they never imagined. What I do know is that the artists behind Hospital of Emotions gave those walls a remarkable new chapter, transforming a place once dedicated to treating the human body into one that invited visitors to explore the human experience.

And maybe that's what I appreciated most. The building didn't need to forget what it had been to become something new. Its past made the art more powerful, and the art gave me a different way of thinking about its past.

I arrived expecting to discover an unusual exhibition in Los Angeles. I left thinking about the countless lives that had intersected inside one building, and the extraordinary creativity of the people who had found a way to make those rooms feel alive again.
