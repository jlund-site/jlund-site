The Night New York
Became Someone Else

An invitation from friends, a float built in a few hours, and a Halloween night that only New York could imagine.

New York has a particular talent for making the extraordinary feel completely normal. On Halloween night, that talent becomes something closer to a civic responsibility. Streets turn into stages, strangers become characters, and for a few hours the city seems to agree that imagination deserves the right of way.

I had been invited by friends who run 99 Brewing Co., a creative agency with exactly the kind of energy you want around when a parade float needs to come together. A few hours before the procession, we were still putting things in place, adjusting details, and working through the small, practical surprises that come with turning an idea into something that can actually travel down a Manhattan street. It was the kind of wonderfully improvised collaboration that reminds me how much of any great experience happens before the audience arrives.

Then the city began to change around us. Costumes appeared in every direction, each one seemingly determined to outdo the last. There were towering pumpkin creatures that looked as though they had escaped from a fantastical garden, an entire gathering of Dolly Partons in pink cowboy hats and blonde wigs, and extraordinary Día de los Muertos celebrations filled with color, music, and theatrical detail. New York does not approach Halloween with restraint. It commits.

From the float, the perspective was different. Instead of watching the spectacle pass by, we were moving through it. The crowds were part of the performance, offering applause, laughter, and that unmistakable New York enthusiasm for anyone willing to make a little magic in public. For someone who has spent much of his life creating experiences for other people, it was a pleasure to simply be inside one.

The evening also offered a perfect detour into movie nostalgia. I stopped at the original Ghostbusters firehouse in Tribeca, the real Hook & Ladder 8, where a familiar doorway can instantly transport you into a fictional New York. Between the firehouse, the Stay Puft Marshmallow Man, and a glimpse of the Ecto 1, the city seemed to be having a conversation with its own cinematic imagination.

What stayed with me was not one particular costume or float. It was the generosity of the whole thing. People had spent hours, perhaps weeks, making something elaborate, funny, strange, or beautiful, simply to share it with people they had never met. My friends had invited me into that spirit, and for one unforgettable night, I got to help carry it down the street. New York gave us a thousand characters. The best part was getting to be one of them.
